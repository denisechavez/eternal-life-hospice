Talking with Children When a Loved One Is Seriously Ill

Honest, gentle, age-aware words help children grieve well. Guidance for one of the hardest conversations a family faces.

← All Journal Posts
By Eternal Life Hospice · June 17, 2026 · 6 min read

When someone in the family is dying, the instinct is often to shield the children from it. Yet children usually sense far more than we think, and what they imagine in the silence is often heavier than the truth. Honest, gentle words — offered at their level — help them grieve in a way that lasts a lifetime.

Use plain, true language

Soft euphemisms can confuse a young child. Phrases like “went to sleep” or “we lost them” can turn bedtime or a crowded store into something frightening. It is kinder, in the long run, to use simple, accurate words: sick, dying, died. They are hard to say. They are also clear, and clarity is its own comfort.

Let their questions lead

You do not need to deliver the whole truth in one conversation. Answer what is actually asked, simply and honestly, and then stop. A child who wants more will ask more, in their own time. “I don’t know” is an honest and perfectly good answer, too.

A gentle openerYou may have noticed Grandpa is very sick. The doctors and nurses are keeping him comfortable, but they can’t make his body better. Do you have any questions about that?

Expect grief to come in waves

Children often grieve in bursts — tears one moment, play the next. This is not denial; it is how young hearts pace themselves. Keeping familiar routines — school, meals, bedtime — gives them steady ground while everything else shifts.

Include them, if they want to be included

Many children find comfort in a small role: drawing a picture, choosing a song, sitting for a few minutes to say hello. Offer the chance without forcing it, and honor a no as readily as a yes.

You don’t have to do this alone. Hospice care includes social workers and counselors who support the whole family — children included — and Medicare-certified hospices offer bereavement support to loved ones for thirteen months after a death. Ask your team; helping families through exactly these moments is part of the work.

Watch for when more help is needed

Some grief is simply grief, and it eases with love and time. But if a child withdraws for a long stretch, cannot sleep, or seems stuck in fear or anger, a counselor who works with grieving children can help. Reaching for that support is a sign of strength, not failure.
