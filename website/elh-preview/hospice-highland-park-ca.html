Planning for Safety and Access in Your Highland Park Home

Living in a historic home in Northeast Los Angeles often means navigating unique architectural features that may impact care. When preparing for hospice services, the primary physical environment where the patient sleeps must be assessed for safety. If the primary living space is located on an upper floor, stairs may present a challenge for both the patient and caregivers when mobility begins to decline.

Assessing your home layout is a vital first step. We encourage families to consider if a ground-floor transition is feasible before starting services. This allows for easier access for clinical team members and reduces the risk of falls for the patient. Thoughtful planning regarding furniture placement and clear pathways ensures the home remains a supportive and safe environment.

Preparing Your Living Space for Care

Begin by identifying which room offers the best balance of patient comfort and ease of access. If the current sleeping area requires navigating stairs, discuss whether moving to a main-floor living room or study might be appropriate for the patient's current energy levels and safety needs.

Consider how the layout affects daily transfers. If a patient requires assistance from a bed to a chair, ensure there is enough physical space for a caregiver to stand comfortably on either side of the bed. Removing rugs, securing cords and ensuring adequate lighting can help prevent tripping hazards during night-time hours.

Finally, identify a quiet space where our nursing team can discuss private care updates with the primary caregiver. Having a clear plan for how furniture might be rearranged allows you to focus on the patient rather than logistical hurdles when our clinical staff arrives for their scheduled assessment visits.

Understanding Your Benefits and Support

Hospice services are designed to manage pain and symptoms related to the terminal illness. Eligibility requires a physician’s certification. Please note that routine room and board in a home or facility setting is typically not covered by the hospice benefit, though specific inpatient or respite circumstances may be assessed.

While the benefit provides clinical support, it does not guarantee continuous staffing or 24-hour monitoring. Families often coordinate with private-duty agencies for personal care needs beyond the hospice scope. There may be limited copayments involved, so it is important to review your specific insurance coverage early in the process.

Hospice election does not mean that every existing medication or treatment must be discontinued. We work with your current clinicians to determine which treatments directly address the goals of comfort and symptom management. Direct all questions regarding changes to medications or ongoing non-hospice treatments to your primary physician.

Common questions about hospice in Highland Park

Will you help me move the furniture in my house?

Our clinical team focuses on identifying safety needs and assessing the environment for the patient's care. While we cannot perform heavy lifting or furniture moving, we can provide guidance on safe room layouts and suggest how to arrange space to improve accessibility and safety during daily care routines.

Can hospice provide 24-hour nursing at home?

Hospice is a visit-based service providing scheduled clinical care and education for family caregivers. It does not provide continuous 24-hour bedside nursing, personal care aides or monitoring. Families often supplement hospice care with private-duty services if constant supervision or intensive hands-on assistance is required in the home.

What if the patient lives on the second floor?

If a patient resides on an upper floor without elevator access, it may pose significant safety risks for both the patient and the caregivers. We recommend discussing a transition to a main-level living space before starting hospice to ensure our clinical team can safely provide the necessary assessment and support.
