## Question 1: "Can a hospice patient still go to a family event — a graduation, a wedding?"

Yes. Absolutely yes.

Hospice is not a facility, in most cases. It's a service that comes to your home. Which means the patient is not confined anywhere. They can attend a graduation. They can go to a wedding. They can be at a family dinner, in their own clothes, with the people they love.

Our team coordinates. If someone needs extra medication for the outing, we plan for that. If there are mobility considerations, we plan for that too.

What hospice protects is quality of life — and quality of life often means being present for the moments that matter.

---

## Question 2: "What if we change our minds and want to try treatment again?"

You can.

The Medicare hospice benefit can be revoked at any time. No penalty. No waiting period. No paperwork burden on the family.

If someone decides they want to pursue a curative treatment, they return to standard Medicare coverage and can pursue that path. And if they later decide hospice is right again, they can re-enroll.

This is not a one-way door. I want families to know that clearly, because I think it keeps some people from making the call they need to make. You are not locked in.

---

## Question 3: "Will someone actually come in the middle of the night?"

Yes.

This is the question I take most seriously, because it's where some hospices fail families and ours does not.

When a nurse says they're available 24/7, that means a nurse answers the phone — not a voicemail, not an answering service that pages someone. A nurse. Who can assess the situation over the phone, who can dispatch someone, who can talk a family through a frightening moment at 3 AM.

That is the standard. And it is what "24/7 access" actually means when it's done right.

---

## Question 4: "What's the difference between hospice and palliative care?"

Both are about comfort. The difference is timing and eligibility.

Palliative care can happen alongside curative treatment at any stage of a serious illness. It adds a layer of symptom management and support while treatment is still the goal.

Hospice is a specific Medicare benefit for people with a terminal prognosis of six months or less who have chosen to focus on comfort over curative treatment.

Many people find their way to hospice from palliative care — when the time is right and the goals shift. They're not opposites. They're often a path.

---

## Question 5: "How do we know when it's time?"

There's no single answer, but there are patterns.

More hospitalizations in the past six months. Weight loss without a clear cause. Increasing pain or discomfort that's harder to manage. Declining function — less ability to walk, to eat, to do the things they love. Exhaustion that rest doesn't fix, for the patient and the family both.

If you're asking this question — if the thought has crossed your mind more than once — that is usually a sign that a conversation is worth having. Not a commitment. A conversation.

We can talk with you before you're sure. That's what the first call is for.

---

## Question 6: "What diagnoses qualify for hospice?"

Most people think hospice is for cancer. It is — but it is far from only that.

Heart failure. COPD. Renal disease. Dementia. ALS. Parkinson's. Liver disease. Stroke. Any terminal diagnosis with a prognosis of six months or less qualifies under Medicare guidelines.

The criterion isn't the diagnosis. It's the prognosis. If a physician has had the conversation about what the timeline may look like, it's worth asking whether hospice is an option.

---

## Question 7: "What does the hospice aide actually do?"

The aide provides personal care — bathing, grooming, dressing, assistance with positioning. The intimate, hands-on care that a family member may not feel equipped to provide, or that has become physically demanding.

It sounds simple. It isn't. Done well, it is an act of profound dignity — treating a person's body with care and attention and gentleness.

Our aides are not there to "help out." They are there to make sure that every morning, the person they're caring for feels cared for. That is specific, skilled, essential work.

---

## Question 8: "How do I talk to my family about wanting hospice for myself?"

Start with the "why" — not the logistics.

Tell them what matters to you. Where you want to be. What you're afraid of. What you want to feel. What you don't want.

The logistics — the paperwork, the benefit, the process — are all manageable once the conversation about values has happened. It's that first conversation that families avoid, because it feels like saying goodbye.

It doesn't have to be. It can be the most important conversation you've ever had — and one you'll all be grateful happened.

---

## Question 9: "What if my loved one refuses hospice?"

We hear this often.

Sometimes people refuse because they don't have accurate information — they believe hospice means giving up, or that it will hasten death. A conversation with a hospice team, or even just educational materials, sometimes shifts that.

Sometimes the refusal is about control — wanting to be the one who decides. In that case, planting the seed and letting it rest is often more effective than pressing.

And sometimes, genuinely, a person isn't ready. We meet people where they are. The door stays open.

What I'd encourage families to do: keep the conversation gentle and factual. Ask what they're afraid of. Then answer that — not the logistics, the fear.

## Question 10: "What makes Eternal Life Hospice different?"

A few things. Let me be specific.

We are ACHC-accredited — a voluntary accreditation that goes beyond state licensing requirements and is held by a small percentage of hospices nationally.

We are physician-led and compliance-driven. Every care plan is individual. Every patient is reviewed daily. Every call is answered by a nurse.

And we offer integrative therapies — sound baths, music at the bedside, aromatherapy, pet therapy, volunteer companionship — as part of the Medicare benefit, at no additional cost.

But the thing I'd point to most is the culture. The morning huddle. The handwritten notes. The way this team talks about the people they're caring for.

You can't certify culture. You can only build it. We've built it.
